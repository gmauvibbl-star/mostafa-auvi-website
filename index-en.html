Novel: Chandradwip

A century of riverine life. The lowlands of Barisal, the Chandradwip tidal zone, where the boundary between land and water is redrawn every day. In this water-world lies a village called Bighai, and around it this tale is born.

The villagers know that the river that gives can also take away. After a storm, after a collapse, standing under the shadow of a dark zamindari, they decide to leave their settlement and float on boats in search of new land. Thirteen boats, thirteen families, and before them an unknown river, an unknown sea, an unknown future. The boatman of this journey is Keramat, and beside him stands Manikjan, whom the river knows in its own language.

Alongside people, this novel walks with folklore, dreams, amulets and land records. Baul songs fill the air, on eclipse nights Hindus and Muslims together recite their own protective mantras, and a boat buried underground wakes. Here the supernatural and the real sit in the same courtyard and share tobacco — no one questions. 1850 to 14 August 1947, this century of history does not appear in speeches. It appears in people's language, their clothing, their fear, their love.

This is the chronicle of a settlement's birth, of a displacement, and of those people who, losing everything, did not lose their voice.

The Writer's Silence and the Reader's Responsibility

For several weeks a question has been pursuing me. When does a writer fall silent, and when does that silence become a responsibility to the reader? Sitting with the manuscript of a new novel, the question becomes clearer, because while writing I feel that some words, once written, are exhausted, and those left unwritten stay awake in the gaps between pages. In a scene where a person stops mid-sentence, the stopping is the real dialogue. In an empty room, a pair of empty slippers by the door says a great deal. I learned this from books, and forgot it again and again.

But a writer's silence is not always art. Sometimes it is fear, sometimes exhaustion, sometimes a grievance that no one will understand my words. Then I cannot distinguish between staying silent and speaking. Here the reader arrives. One who sits beyond the page and fills my unspoken portion with their own life. What the writer leaves, the reader takes. This exchange has a responsibility, and it belongs to both.

In this writing I share some of that conversation with the reader. Because a book is never only the writer's. Until it is printed, it is mine; after that, it belongs to those who read it, misunderstand it, love it in their own way, and perhaps one night suddenly think — this thought was mine. If the writer's silence can leave space for that moment, then the silence has meaning. And the reader's responsibility is perhaps just this: to sit quietly in that empty space.
