Novel: Chandradwip

The chronicle of a hundred years of riverine life. The lowlands of Barisal, the tidal reaches of Chandradwip, where the line between earth and water is drawn anew each day. In this land of water there is a village called Bighai, and around that village this tale is born.

The people of the village know that the river, which gives, also knows how to take back. After one storm, after one erosion, standing beneath the dark shadow of a zamindari estate, they resolve to leave their settlement and drift in boats in search of new land. Thirteen boats, thirteen families, and before them an unknown river, an unknown sea, an unknown future. The helmsman of this journey is Keramat, and beside him stands Manikjan, whom the river knows in its own language.

In this novel, walking alongside the people are folk belief, dreams, amulets and rent-rolls. Gatherings form to the songs of the bayati, on eclipse nights Hindus and Muslims murmur their own protective charms side by side, and boats buried beneath the earth stir awake. Here the miraculous and the everyday sit in the same courtyard and share a pipe of tobacco; no one questions the other. The history stretching from 1880 to 1947 enters no lecture hall. It arrives in human speech, in their clothing, in their fears, in their loves.

This is the chronicle of a hamlet's birth, the chronicle of a displacement, and the chronicle of those people who, having lost everything, did not lose their voice.
