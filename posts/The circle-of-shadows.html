The Circle of Shadows

Mostafa Auvi (মোস্তফা অভি) · 2026-11-04

The ash-grey clouds had settled over the small town well before dusk. In the distance, the brass bells and gong of the evening aarti were ringing at the Kali temple. Beneath that monotonous metallic sound, I felt a faint, nagging pull somewhere below my ribs.

Shahriyar had come home unusually early that day. It was not like him to return before nightfall. He entered through the front door and walked into the first room of the house. The dark lines etched across his face made my chest tighten. I could tell at once that something had happened to my husband. But before I could ask, he lowered his eyes. Without a word, he went into the bedroom, pushed his shoes across the floor and lay down on the bed.

Several times I asked him to wash his hands and face and come to eat. In the kitchen, the pot of rice was boiling furiously over the clay stove. Apart from the steady hiss of the rising steam, there was no other sound in the house. After a long while, I quietly entered the bedroom. Shahriyar was lying on his side at one corner of the bed. Tears were slipping from the corners of his eyes and soaking the pillow. I stood motionless by the door. In twelve years of marriage, through family bereavements and heavy business losses, I had never seen this man's voice falter. Yet here he was, lying alone and weeping, while I stood in the doorway, unable to think of anything to say. I could not remember ever seeing such tears in his eyes.

The last trace of light caught in the window curtain grew dim and disappeared. Shahriyar had always been disciplined and devout. No matter how crowded the market was on market days, he never missed the Asr prayer. In this subdistrict town, he owned a wholesale rice depot and a large grocery shop. The previous year, he had also ventured into the steel rod and cement trade. When I first came to live with him, we had struggled to make ends meet. Even in those days of hardship, however, there had been a quiet understanding between us. Now, when I thought of those early years, they seemed to belong to someone else's life. With time, poverty had loosened its grip, and we had been blessed with a son and a daughter. I had also grown accustomed to the casual deference a wealthy merchant's wife receives in a neighbourhood. At the vegetable market, sellers would tuck the best produce beneath the rest in my basket. The women in the courtyard admired the folds of my cotton saris. After Friday prayers, the imam of the main mosque would stand talking to Shahriyar a little longer than he did with others. I had never considered these things special privileges. I simply believed they were my due as Shahriyar's wife.

Much later, in the stillness of the room, Shahriyar told me in a very low voice that he wanted to marry again.

The moment the words left his mouth, I felt as though the cement floor beneath me had split down the middle. A wordless fear closed around my throat. He was the one person I had always relied on, the familiar presence that had accompanied every day of my life. His presence had become so deeply woven into my existence, like the small flame of an oil lamp burning beside a bed in a dark room, that I had never imagined him moving elsewhere. After he went into the other room, I put out the lamp and lay alone in bed. Memories of our earlier days began returning, one after another.

In those first years of hardship, he had once brought home a coarse handloom sari from somewhere and placed it in my lap. “Wait a little longer,” he had said. “Once the business gets established, I'll bring you a Dhakai jamdani.” As I stood holding the sari, he rested his sweat-damp forehead against my hair and told me of a wonderful dream he had. The sari was reddish brown, with a wild, tangled pattern worked in white thread along its border. It still lay tightly folded on the lowest shelf of the wardrobe. I had never worn it. Whenever I thought of putting it on, the smell of our old poverty seemed to rise from its coarse fabric. But that night I felt that the sari might be the only silent document of our marriage, a reminder that there had once been a time when Shahriyar belonged to me alone.

I could not understand how he had found the courage to ask my permission for something so unjust. The woman he wanted to bring into our home was, I had heard, a recently widowed mother of two young children. Her circumstances were so desperate that she could not even be sure there would be rice in the cooking pot at noon. It was hardly surprising that a helpless woman should go looking for shelter. But the tears in Shahriyar's eyes told me that this was more than compassion for a widow's suffering. He had never been a man tender-hearted enough to weep over the misfortunes of a stranger. Who could know that better than I?

Much later, I heard from others that the woman's name was Asma. Her husband had worked as a labourer on the trucks used in Shahriyar's rod and cement business. His job was to load rods, cement and other goods onto the trucks at the wholesale depots and unload them at the shops. He had died in a road accident. After his death, Asma came one day to Shahriyar's depot to settle the account of the money still owed to her husband. She sat on the wooden platform at the back of the depot and wept uncontrollably. They said that was the first time Shahriyar had lost his place in the business ledger. At the time, of course, I had no way of knowing these details. All I understood was that a room had opened inside my husband's mind, a room someone else was now seeking to enter.

I made him get up from the bed and stand facing me. I kept wondering why he had become so desperate to marry this woman. There were so many other ways to help a destitute family. He could send money every month in secret. He could provide rice and lentils. Why did it have to be marriage? People went hungry in this town every day. I had never once seen him sit on his prayer mat and shed tears for them. I asked him how far things had gone.

Shahriyar did not answer. He turned away and stared at the whitewashed wall. His gaze remained fixed on the plaster, as though he were standing before me while his thoughts had travelled somewhere far away. His silence burned inside me. At any other time, even a minor disagreement would have made him angry enough to say something harsh. Today there was no trace of anger on his face, only a kind of weary indifference. Eventually, I left the room and bolted the door of the adjoining one. As I took off the glass bangles from my wrists and placed them on the table, I felt that each one contained the accumulated weariness of twelve years of marriage.

The thought that I would have to share my own husband with another woman numbed every part of me. His touch, the whispers in the night, his little jokes and familiar smile, the habits we had built over so many years—would someone else now have a claim to all of them? I could not bear the thought. A bitter taste, like the leaves of the nishinda plant, settled beneath my tongue.

What had I not done for this household? I had been his lover, his caretaker, his cook, the mother of his two children. I had carried every one of those roles on my own. Even when poverty was at its worst and the rice container stood empty, I had gone without a meal and stood beside him with a smile. Had he forgotten all that so easily? The man who had once been too reserved even to look another man's wife in the eye while speaking to her was now lost in thoughts of a widow.

I stood before the cloudy mirror of the dressing table and studied my face for a long time. Dark patches had appeared beneath my eyes, and my cheeks no longer had their former firmness. The changes motherhood had brought to my body seemed, that night, almost like a personal failing. Looking at myself in the mirror, I felt that twelve years of marriage had passed over my face without my ever keeping count.

All this time, I had assumed that our household, our bed and the familiar rhythm of our days would continue unchanged for ever. It had never occurred to me that any of it might come to an end. Shahriyar's years of prayers and fasting, his constant concern for our well-being—all of it suddenly seemed like a mask. A fire began burning inside my chest, fierce as glowing coal. No argument could put it out.

The next morning, when Shahriyar still did not come out of the room, I said nothing. I took the children by the hand and set off for my father's house. As we crossed the front door, my elder daughter looked up at me in bewilderment and asked, “Isn't Abbu coming, Ma?”

I did not answer. I simply held her hand tightly and climbed into the rickshaw. Throughout the journey, she kept lifting the curtain of the rickshaw hood to look at the familiar shops along the road, the electric poles and the dusty neem trees. From her expression, I could tell she was trying to memorise every detail. Perhaps she could not understand why we were leaving home. Yet there was such intensity in her gaze, as though she sensed that we might never again travel this road so casually.

The days in the small, damp room at my father's house passed like a sentence of confinement. Time seemed unwilling to move. Morning came, noon slipped into afternoon, and then night, but each day remained as heavy as the one before. Fifteen days passed. Then, one afternoon, while the fierce sunlight was bleaching the courtyard earth, Shahriyar suddenly appeared and stood at the threshold of my father's house.

My elder brother was furious with him. He could not accept that such a deep rift had opened in his sister's marriage without his knowing anything about it. But when the children saw their father in the courtyard, they ran towards him with delight. Those fifteen days apart had softened something inside me too. From where I stood, half-hidden, I saw Shahriyar's sunburnt, drawn face, and a small pang of tenderness stirred in my chest.

Still, I did not go up to him and weep. In a firm voice, I asked only, “How are you?”

He kept looking into my eyes. The exhaustion gathered around them made me want, for a moment, to say, “Come, let's forget everything and go home.” But then another thought came to me: perhaps his drawn face had nothing to do with missing me. Perhaps he was simply frustrated that he could not bring the widow into his home. I was about to walk past him when he suddenly caught my hand.

“I've put all those thoughts behind me. Come, let's take the children and go home.”

At his words, a wave of joy swept through my whole body. I thought of nothing else. I did not even take the time to pack our clothes. I left with him without eating. As I was leaving, my mother placed her hand on my head. Her face was drawn and solemn as she said, “In a marriage, my daughter, sometimes you have to bow your head. But don't let it sink so far into the dust that you can never raise it again.”

When we returned home, Shahriyar seemed to have truly changed. He spoke to me as naturally as he once had. At night, the warmth of our old affection returned to our bed. In the dim, flickering fluorescent light, his face once again looked familiar and trustworthy. On market days, he would secretly bring home a small bottle of perfume or a packet of Senora sanitary napkins wrapped in brown paper and tuck it beneath the bed. For the children, he brought chocolates and new exercise books. Sometimes he would make a mischievous remark in his old familiar way, and the heaviness in the house would lift. It seemed that peace had returned to our household.

Seeing this gentler side of Shahriyar, I too gradually tried to put Asma out of my mind. I never wanted to know what had eventually become of the widow and her young children. Somewhere inside me, I even felt a cruel satisfaction that I had managed to reclaim what was mine. Now and then, on my way to the market, I would glimpse from behind the hood of my rickshaw a tall woman wrapped in a shawl, walking at the far end of the road with a child on her hip. I would quickly turn my eyes away. I felt that if I looked at her, the dust of her misfortune might settle on me.

After that, Shahriyar never mentioned taking a second wife again. I grew complacent in my victory. I believed I had brought my husband back to me, and that our bond would never loosen again. I did not realise that the most secure days of my life were already slipping away, like a sandbank slowly crumbling beneath the water.

One day, at the beginning of winter, Shahriyar returned from the rice depot and said that his body felt strangely numb and that the muscles at the back of his head were tightening. I assumed it was because he had spent too long standing in the harsh sun at the market and paid little attention. A few days later, as we sat down to dinner, he complained of a heavy pressure in his chest, like a stone pressing down from within. He said he ought to see a good doctor.

I gave a faint smile and brushed his concern aside. “Once you're past forty, everyone gets a little rheumatic pain now and then. I'll take you to the district hospital next Friday.”

But that Friday never came. Even now, whenever I remember how carelessly I dismissed his complaints, I shudder.

It was the last night of Poush. I was sitting on the veranda, making bhapa pitha from soft rice-flour dough. The end of my bluish cotton sari lay trailing across the dusty floor. The exposed nape of my neck glistened with sweat in the stove's fierce red glow. Suddenly, I looked up and saw Shahriyar sitting at the edge of the bed, staring at me without blinking. I recognised the hidden hunger in his eyes. Still, I lowered my head and pretended to concentrate on my cooking. The soft scent of the dough, the warmth of the stove and the fog outside came together to make a night when time seemed reluctant to move forward, content simply to stand still.

Perhaps my delay had become unbearable to his restless body. Suddenly, the wooden bed creaked. Then he called out to me in a broken voice.

“Something inside my chest feels terribly empty. I can't breathe.”

I smiled at him. “Just let me take the pitha pot off the stove. I'll be right there. Everything will be all right.”

But before I could finish speaking, a torrent of vomit poured from his mouth. His face turned deathly pale, and he collapsed onto the bed. The children sitting beside him screamed in terror. Trembling, Shahriyar reached out and stroked their heads one last time, trying to say something indistinct. Before I could make out his words, his eyes rolled back. I sent my son running to call the neighbours and gathered Shahriyar's head into my lap. He vomited once more, and then his neck went limp. Something ice-cold seemed to pass through my entire body.

On that fog-shrouded night, despite all my cries, Shahriyar settled his accounts with the world and departed. In one corner of the veranda, the uncooked rice-flour dough still lay where I had left it. Later, it dried and hardened like wood. For many months, I could not bring myself to clear it away.

His sudden death shattered the household I had so carefully built. The man on whom I had relied so completely that I had never felt the need to look closely at the world beyond our home was gone in the blink of an eye. During the day, I sat like stone amid the crowd of relatives. At night, alone in bed, I buried my face in the pillow and wept. But a reality harsher than grief was waiting for me.

On the day of his janazah, beyond the mosque compound, I caught sight of a veiled face behind a distant mango tree. Asma. Her eyes were lowered towards the ground, and her body trembled in silence. No one in the crowd seemed to recognise her or even tried to find out who she was. I recognised her at once. Strangely, I felt neither anger nor jealousy that day. Only an immense weariness settled over me. It seemed that two women, standing on opposite banks, had both been left destitute by the same man, yet neither could reach out and touch the other's hand.

After Shahriyar's death, I had neither the knowledge nor the strength of mind to take charge of the business. The employees brought me a small amount of money at the end of each month, and somehow I managed to put rice and lentils on the table. I felt no need to check the ledgers or match the invoices against the accounts. In the midst of grief, such calculations seemed meaningless. But it did not take long for me to pay the price for that neglect.

One day, the cement company's dealer arrived and dropped a bundle of red vouchers on the table. He told me that Shahriyar had been taking stock for the past year and selling it elsewhere without depositing a single paisa with the company. I listened in disbelief. The debt was so enormous that even selling our entire house would not be enough to repay it. Soon, the goods in the depot began disappearing. Then, under pressure from the creditors, I tried to sell the house.

That was when Asma's face flashed through my mind. Could Shahriyar have been diverting money from the business to help her in secret? Once the question entered my mind, I could not get rid of it. As I struggled to settle the debts, our carefully built home was put up for auction. One day, we had to leave the house where I had lived so securely for so many years, the house whose every room I knew by heart.

Before leaving, I was sorting through some old papers when I came across a rent receipt. It recorded the monthly rent for a tin-roofed room in a deserted lane of the town. Shahriyar's signature was at the bottom. The name and address left little room for doubt. Perhaps, until his final breath, he had wanted to keep both families afloat. But he had been unable to bring either of them safely to shore.

With no other choice, I took the children and moved into my elder brother's household in the town. It did not take long to realise that our three extra mouths had become a burden on that middle-class family. My brother left for the office every morning with a grim expression. My sister-in-law's words grew sharper by the day. Even after spending the entire day doing the household chores, I felt like an outsider in that home.

I desperately needed a job. But I knew almost nothing about how the world beyond the four walls of a household worked. The little education I had managed to acquire after leaving school was not enough to qualify me for any job. My sister-in-law never said anything directly. Yet her irritation was plain in the bowls of vegetables and plates of rice she served me each day. She had once said, “This is your home too.” Now she whispered to my brother behind my back, wondering how many mouths one salary could feed in a market like this.

It grew harder and harder to keep living under that roof, swallowing my humiliation. I tried to endure the daily indignities and made sure the children noticed nothing. At night, after they had fallen asleep, I thought of Shahriyar. I had spent so many years with him, and now the home, the business and the security he had left behind were all gone. I had to ask for a place in someone else's house. Sometimes I thought that if Shahriyar had been alive, things might never have come to this. Then, a moment later, I remembered how much had remained hidden in our marriage even before his death.

The days continued to weigh upon me like a stone on my chest. No morning brought any new hope. Each one only reminded me of the happy days I had spent with Shahriyar. Then, one morning, my elder brother called me into his bedroom.

In an entirely matter-of-fact voice, he told me that a senior officer in his office was looking for a bride. The man was considerably older than I was. He held a senior position in a government department and was respected, wealthy and pleasant in manner. My brother wanted me to agree without hesitation to become the man's second wife.

I needed a secure roof over my head and the children's. Yet the thought of their uncertain future brought me no comfort. For a mother, the faces of her children are a far more compelling reason to go on living than her own need for security. The children looked at me anxiously, as though there were a question in their eyes to which I had no answer. I felt they sensed that some great change was waiting just ahead.

I told my brother at once that I did not consent to the marriage. He said the gentleman was willing to take my children into his household as well. I wanted to be certain. I insisted on meeting the man face to face before making any decision. My brother was deeply annoyed. He considered it unreasonable for a widowed sister to impose conditions on a decision made by her guardian. But I refused to agree otherwise.

Two days later, I sat opposite the older gentleman in the drawing room of my brother's house. In a calm voice, I asked him to explain clearly what he intended for my children's future and education. As we spoke, I realised that he had little genuine concern for the crisis I was facing. Instead, his eyes lingered with an unmistakable hunger on the edge of my cotton sari and the glistening line of sweat gathered in the opening at the neck of my blouse.

Feeling insulted, I began to rise from my seat. He hurriedly tried to reassure me, promising that he would gladly take full responsibility for my children.

I sat there stunned, thinking about the strange turns of fate. To survive as a vulnerable woman in this society, I needed someone who could shelter me. I needed some assurance I could lean on, something that would allow me to get my children through the days ahead. I had buried the claims of my own body and youth long ago. But I had no power to deny the demands of food and shelter.

I had learned that the gentleman's first wife had been confined to her bed for many years with a severe form of arthritis. It was not difficult to understand what lay behind his proposal. I needed him simply as a means of survival. He needed me to satisfy the secret physical hunger of his unfulfilled years. To keep myself alive, I was about to become the object of a stranger's nocturnal desire. The knowledge crushed me from within.

That evening, as the rickshaw jolted along the road on my way back to my brother's house, I suddenly remembered that ash-grey evening from so many years ago. Shahriyar had come home and wept. Then, in a very low voice, he had told me he wanted to marry again. I had asked why he needed to marry a helpless woman in order to help her. He had given no answer. In anger, I had taken the children and gone to my father's house. Fifteen days later, he came and said he had put all those thoughts behind him. I believed him and returned home. Then, secure in my victory, I tried to forget Asma.

Outside the rickshaw hood, the evening light was fading. One by one, the shops along the road were switching on their lights. I realised that after all these years, I had become Asma myself. I was about to enter another woman's household as a second wife. Perhaps, when she learned of my arrival, that ailing wife would sit alone in her dark room, weeping into her pillow, just as I had once done.

I know, of course, that life does not move in a straight line. Sometimes it is more like a circle, whose beginning and end are impossible to distinguish. Only the positions people occupy change with time. Once, I was the wife who tried to prevent another woman from entering her household. Now I was that other woman, whose presence might bring darkness into someone else's carefully ordered life.

The rickshaw jolted on down the road. I did not look back.
